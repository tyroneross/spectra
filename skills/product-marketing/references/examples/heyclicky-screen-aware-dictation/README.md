# HeyClicky — screen-aware dictation launch

**Assessment:** This is a strong founder-led feature launch because the person
explains the meaning while the product proves each claim in real software. The
edit does not tour product chrome. It shows HeyClicky changing an outcome inside
Apple Notes, Gmail, Claude Code, the Skills Library, and Google Slides.

- Source: [Farza on X](https://x.com/farzatv/status/2077130366230639022)
- Published: 2026-07-14
- Format: 2:00, 16:9, 29.97 fps
- Archived assets: [video](video.mp4) · [poster](poster.jpg) · [storyboard](storyboard.jpg) · [metadata](metadata.json)

## Story arc

The video uses a **familiar baseline -> devalue the baseline -> reveal the
differentiator -> prove it in escalating contexts** arc. That contrast is more
specific than a generic problem/solution explainer.

| Time | Beat | Person or product | What the beat does |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:12 | Hook and mechanic | Founder, then shortcut close-up | Names dictation immediately and shows `fn + control` within four seconds. |
| 0:12–0:22 | Familiar baseline | Apple Notes | Proves ordinary streaming dictation with a concrete three-item task list. |
| 0:22–0:31 | Honest contrast | Founder | Admits that many products already dictate, then names the deeper problem: the user may not know what to write. |
| 0:31–0:38 | Differentiated reveal | Founder | Introduces screen-aware dictation only after the commodity behavior is understood. |
| 0:38–0:57 | Flagship proof | Gmail intercut with founder | Turns an existing email into a reply, asks for a specific tone and question, then shows the generated draft in the user's voice. |
| 0:57–1:08 | Meaning | Founder | Explains why the result matters: screen context plus conversational writing. |
| 1:08–1:21 | Second proof | Claude Code intercut with founder | Converts non-technical uncertainty into a useful follow-up prompt and a learning-oriented response. |
| 1:21–1:39 | Platform expansion | Skills Library and Google Slides | Activates a Y Combinator skill and rewrites a pitch-deck opener, showing that skills extend the core behavior. |
| 1:39–1:52 | Breadth | Founder | Names Google Ads, resumes, Slack, and LinkedIn as adjacent use cases. |
| 1:52–1:58 | Candid close | Founder | Says the feature is live, acknowledges bugs, and asks for feedback. |
| 1:58–2:00 | Brand stamp | Animated wordmark | Ends on `heyClicky` after the product has earned attention. |

## Person versus product

Measured screen share:

- Founder talking head: **64.7s / 53.9%**
- Product and host-app UI: **51.0s / 42.4%**
- Physical shortcut close-ups: **2.6s / 2.2%**
- End card: **1.8s / 1.5%**

The founder carries interpretation, trust, enthusiasm, and the candid limitation.
The product carries mechanics and proof. The edit returns to the founder after
each screen sequence to answer “why does this matter?” instead of leaving the
viewer to interpret a silent screen recording.

The strongest choice is that HeyClicky itself remains visually ambient. Most of
the proof happens inside tools the audience already understands. A compact
listening indicator, a red activity/cursor marker, and the Skills Library are the
only recurring product surfaces. This makes the value proposition “works where
you already work,” not “learn another application.”

## What the product footage shows

1. A physical `fn + control` shortcut establishes a repeatable trigger.
2. Apple Notes proves fast, literal speech-to-text before the differentiated claim.
3. Gmail shows screen context, an intent-level request, a short generation wait,
   and a completed reply.
4. Claude Code shows the same interaction working in a technical environment and
   makes the output legible long enough to verify it.
5. The Skills Library and a Y Combinator skill show extensibility.
6. Google Slides shows the product acting on a real pitch deck rather than a
   clean-room demo document.

Each demonstration shows input and output. None becomes a feature-menu tour.

## Visual treatment and background imagery

- The founder sits outdoors at a concrete table with a laptop. Green foliage,
  parked cars, warm buildings, and shallow depth of field make the launch feel
  lived-in rather than staged in a studio.
- A red insulated bottle creates a stable accent against black clothing, a black
  laptop, and the green/brown background.
- Two talking-head framings and several jump cuts add motion without changing the
  location or breaking continuity.
- Product recordings appear as a centered Mac desktop/window over a softly visible
  office environment. Desert wallpaper, the macOS menu bar, and the Dock preserve
  context while keeping the active app readable.
- Large white burned-in captions sit in the lower third with a dark shadow. They
  make the video understandable without sound, although they occasionally cover
  the bottom of the product UI.
- The edit uses real UI, cursor movement, captions, and the final wordmark. It
  avoids abstract animation and decorative feature cards.

## Pacing and sound

- The generated transcript contains about **453 words**, or **~230 WPM** before
  the end card. This is faster than a typical explainer and creates launch energy,
  but it also raises comprehension risk.
- Scene analysis detected **37 major visual transitions** across 120 seconds:
  about **18.5 transitions per minute**, with a **2.2s median hold**.
- The audio master measures **-15.3 LUFS integrated**, **4.7 LU loudness range**,
  and **-0.5 dBFS true peak**. Only the last 0.35 seconds register as silence.
- macOS Sound Analysis consistently classified both speech and music. Its strongest
  music labels were plucked strings/mandolin with light mallet percussion, chimes,
  and percussion. The bed feels bright and playful; it becomes most exposed under
  the logo after the speech ends.
- A rhythm estimate clustered near **128–133 BPM**, but speech contaminates that
  measurement; treat it as directional, not a track specification.

The audio never becomes a separate storytelling layer. Speech supplies the
information, the music supplies forward motion, and the visual cuts carry emphasis.

## Why this works

1. **The demonstration starts before the explanation expands.** The shortcut
   arrives at 0:04 and the first real app at 0:12.
2. **The creator earns the differentiator.** He first proves ordinary dictation,
   then openly says it is not unique, which makes the screen-aware reveal credible.
3. **Every claim gets contextual proof.** The viewer sees the surrounding email,
   terminal output, pitch deck, request, and result.
4. **Use cases escalate.** Notes proves mechanics; Gmail proves personal context;
   Claude Code proves reasoning support; skills prove extensibility.
5. **The founder remains human.** Gestures, natural light, conversational delivery,
   and the admission that the feature is buggy create trust without lowering the
   perceived ambition.
6. **Captions make sound optional.** The spoken story survives feed autoplay.

## What to borrow — and what to improve

Borrow:

- Show the trigger in the first five seconds.
- Establish the familiar behavior before claiming the novel behavior.
- Use a person for meaning and real product states for proof.
- Demonstrate the product inside recognizable work, not on an empty demo canvas.
- Return to the person between proof blocks to reset attention.
- End with one candid statement that lowers skepticism.

Improve for a Spectra-produced version:

- Reduce the spoken pace toward 160–180 WPM or cut this into a 60–90 second launch
  asset plus separate use-case clips.
- Keep captions above the active product controls and Dock.
- Replace the broad feedback request with one measurable CTA.
- Demonstrate one of the final breadth claims instead of listing four unproven uses.
- Add a brief pause around the differentiated claim so the viewer can process it.

## Method and limits

The source video was downloaded from the attributed X post on 2026-07-15. The
analysis used scene-boundary detection, frame inspection, generated speech
transcription checked against burned-in captions, `ffprobe`, EBU R128 loudness
analysis, and Apple's built-in Sound Analysis classifier. Scene boundaries and BPM
are estimates; the story and product observations were verified visually.

The repository copy is a 1080p H.264/AAC transcode of the 4K source to keep the
reference reviewable without adding the 157 MB source file. The creator retains all
rights; this copy is preserved for attributed internal product-marketing study.
